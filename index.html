Antebellum Era Burn Scars

Between 1812 and 1861, in the Southeastern United States, planters and settlers used fire not just as a tool for land management, but as a weapon of displacement. These intentional burns were part of broader efforts to expel Indigenous populations from their ancestral homelands—often in preparation for cotton agriculture under the plantation economy. Burning forests and grasslands destroyed food sources, sacred sites, and living space, driving Native communities away under increasingly hostile conditions.

The Muscogee (Creek), Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole peoples, among others, were systematically removed through tactics including legal manipulation, military force, and environmental destruction. Fires intentionally set by settlers and planters made entire regions uninhabitable, and this practice was often coordinated with militia activity and land speculation.

The Tallapoosa Valley in Alabama, for instance, saw repeated burning campaigns during the 1830s, designed to evict the Muscogee people. In South Carolina's Pee Dee River region, planters burned forests to displace the Catawba and Pee Dee people. These tactics laid the groundwork for events like the Trail of Tears, during which thousands of Indigenous people died due to forced relocation.
